# Energy → Water

Collins Park Water Treatment and Bay View Water Reclamation are modeled as high-sensitivity electricity dependencies on the regional grid interface. Their public descriptions establish critical treatment functions, but do not provide facility MW demand, backup duration, feeder topology, or outage consequences. Those unknowns remain explicit.

# Energy → Materials

Materion Elmore and Martin Marietta Woodville are modeled as energy-relevant process loads. Reliable electricity is a high/moderate functional dependency; process heat, fuel split, and demand are not quantified. Elmore remains advanced processing of nonlocal feed, not extraction.

# Energy → Compute

The documented Oppidan Bowling Green 5 MW project is modeled as a planned/unverified compute load requiring grid electricity. No hyperscaler, national-lab-scale facility, operating status, or additional AI data center is inferred.

# Fuel → Generation

Oregon Clean Energy Center, Troy Energy, and Fremont Energy Center receive generalized external natural-gas supply dependencies. EIA documents the distinct supply and pipeline-delivery components of gas purchasing, but no local contract, pipeline, capacity, or interruption probability is assigned. Monroe receives a qualified external coal/petcoke fuel-logistics dependency.

# Water → Generation

Cooling/water is a high qualitative dependency for the two nuclear nodes and a moderate generalized thermal dependency for Monroe and the three gas assets. NRC supports the nuclear cooling relationship; plant-specific intake, withdrawal, discharge, and permit behavior are not modeled.

# Weather → Generation

Bowling Green Wind, Bowling Green Solar, and Temperance Solar receive weather/resource dependencies. DOE/EIA material supports daily, seasonal, and weather-driven variability; this phase does not forecast production or capacity factors.

# Storage → Grid

Bowling Green and Slocum storage are represented as grid-support capabilities. Their duration, state of charge, dispatch, and ability to cover prolonged generation loss are unknown; storage is not treated as indefinite replacement capacity.

# Monroe Transition

Monroe remains an operating regional coal asset with a 3,066 MW EIA coal-capacity basis and a documented planned-retirement transition. This is a dependency-concentration and transition issue, not a calculated deficit. No replacement generation or future grid redistribution is modeled.

# Dependency Concentrations

These are potential resilience concerns, not proven bottlenecks:

- critical drinking-water and wastewater functions share a high electricity dependency;
- multiple thermal assets share fuel and cooling dependencies;
- Monroe’s large planned-retirement transition deserves separate planning evidence;
- storage presence is documented but duration is unresolved;
- the planned compute project would add a continuous-power dependency if it enters service;
- generalized communications support regional grid coordination.

For stronger analysis, facility load measurements, backup-system specifications, fuel contracts/inventory, cooling permits, storage-duration data, compute interconnection/commissioning records, and utility topology would be required.

# Data Gaps

Feeder and substation topology, private load curves, SCADA, transfer limits, contingency models, outage statistics, restoration times, storage state of charge, and the operating status of the Oppidan project remain unavailable or intentionally out of scope.

# Implications for Phase 3C

Phase 3C could examine explicitly fictional future energy states only after a new scenario ledger is approved. It should not backfill the unresolved factual gaps with invented capacity, routes, reliability metrics, or replacement resources.

## Worldbuilding Implications

The factual pattern supports themes of electricity as hidden infrastructure beneath water security; centralized generation versus distributed resilience; fuel dependence versus electrification; large-generation retirement as transition pressure; strategic industry and compute competing for reliable power; limited-duration storage; water/energy circular dependence; and energy/grid information as a governance or security asset. These are interpretive possibilities, not baseline findings.
