E = evidence/model basis; S = scenario condition; K = narrative/character sketch; C = canon.
Everything in this document is **K**. No name, age, household, history, preference, or
relationship here is canon, and none of it changes an inherited packet status. FT-01/02/03/04/10
retain S; FT-11/12 retain K. No E or C is created, and no Phase 1–16 protected artifact,
Phase 17A product, or Phase 17B packet is modified.

## What this document is, and is not

This is character development, not canonization. Six candidate recurring viewpoints are sketched
from six already-approved Phase 17C roles (ROLE-01, 02, 03, 04, 05, 08). They are **not** final
protagonists, not a selected cast, and not a ranked set. The Western Basin remains legible
without any of them; other staff, owners, laboratories, employers, households, and authorities
continue to exist outside this selection.

Construction follows the Phase 17C order — PLACE → SYSTEM → ROLE → ROUTINE → DEPENDENCY →
PRESSURE → CHOICE → CONSEQUENCE. The world was not invented to fit these people. Each profile
derives from exactly one approved role, uses only the authority, dependency, and region fields
already recorded for that role, and adds nothing institutional that the matrix does not support.
No new role category, no new narrative thread, no scene, and no Phase 17D work appears here.

## Reading rules

- Epistemic boundaries govern **world truth**, not what a character believes. A viewpoint may
  overestimate a tool, distrust correct advice, protect income or time, delay an uncomfortable
  issue, or make a defensible choice with an unwanted consequence. Those beliefs stay K and
  never convert a mistaken or interested inference into an accepted scientific claim.
- Competence is ordinary as often as it is technical. Not everyone is a systems expert,
  institutionally fluent, or articulate about infrastructure.
- Regions are interpretive and overlapping, not jurisdictions or service territories.
  Black Swamp Country is interpretive only, and **no held Great Black Swamp geometry is used**.
- Ages are provisional life stages, not exact ages; households are provisional structures, not
  demographic forecasts. Names are ordinary regional-plausible names, carry no demographic
  meaning, and do not predict future population composition. Every viewpoint carries one
  provisional name and two alternates; none is treated as canon.
- No race, ethnicity, ancestry, religion, sexuality, or disability is introduced for any of the
  six. Where a detail of that kind might have been used for variety, it was omitted rather than
  invented, because nothing in the accepted material requires it.

# CHAR-K-01 — Nadine Kowalski

Source role: ROLE-01 — Water utility shift operator
Primary region: Lake Erie Energy & Security Coast / Glass City Core
Status: K
Provisional name: Nadine Kowalski · Alternates: Ellen Brauer, Marcus Dehn

## Core identity

Mid-career, in her forties. She works as a shift operator at a generalized shoreline utility
interface where intake, treatment rooms, service routes, and the working-waterfront edge meet.
Her household is her partner and one teenager; they live in a rented upper flat in the urban
core, twenty minutes from the gate. Employment is stable waged utility work with
shift premiums, and she is, in broad terms, **economically stable but time-poor**: the money is
adequate and the hours are not hers to choose. She qualified through operator certification and
on-the-job training rather than a degree.

## What she is good at

- Reading a shift: comparing overnight readings, sample records, and weather notes against each
  other and noticing which ones cover different times or different media.
- Knowing when two records are **not** comparable, and saying so in a form another shift can use.
- Manual discipline — taking a sample by hand, writing it in the logbook, and keeping the paper
  procedure next to the display.
- Keeping a handoff short and usable under time pressure.
- Working a phone tree: knowing which unit answers at 04:00 and which one will not.

## What she routinely gets wrong

- She treats the log as the record of what happened. Written entries feel complete to her, so a
  condition nobody wrote down is easy for her to underweight.
- She dislikes unresolved ambiguity. At handoff she will sometimes press an untidy question into
  a tidy sentence, which is honest in substance but narrower than the evidence.
- She assumes other units' procedures resemble hers, and is surprised when a contractor's
  permitted visit is scoped differently from a utility work order.
- Under a public-communication deadline she is tempted to endorse a simpler conclusion than her own
  comparison supports.

## Workday

She is on the board at 05:30 ready to take the shift. Gate, badge, a walk past the notice board,
then the first hour is reading: overnight readings, a flagged probe, a lake observation, and the
treatment log. She marks the probe for cleaning, notes the time stamps do not line up, and does
not resolve it. Mid-morning she walks the shoreline approach in a rain shell, checks a cabinet
seal and a gauge beside the display, and radios for a cable replacement. A technician arrives,
which means waiting, coffee from the thermos, and a conversation about who signs the work order.
Lunch is eaten standing up near the railings, watching wind move across the open water. The
afternoon is the handoff: rewriting her notes into the log, marking the unresolved comparison
with a request for a targeted check, and leaving the paper procedure where the next shift will
find it. She does not prepare anything resembling a conclusion. She signs off at 17:45 and rides
home past wet concrete and pump vibration, planning dinner around whether her partner is on a late
shift too.

## Life outside the system

She is on a Thursday bowling league she joined for the two hours of not thinking, and she repairs
secondhand bicycles for neighborhood kids out of a shared garage bay, mostly because a functioning
bike is cheap to give away. She cooks a large Sunday meal whether or not anyone comes.

## Personal pressure

Practical: her mother's clinic appointments are scheduled in a system that does not care about a
rotating roster, and every appointment is a swap with someone else's weekend.
Relationship: her partner has stopped complaining about the shifts and started making plans
without her, which she finds harder to argue with.
Internal contradiction: her job is partly to keep a question open, and she wants very badly to
close it. She defends the roster as fair while quietly believing the relief staffing is not.

## Authority and dependence

She can adjust assigned routines within approved procedures and escalate an observation. She
cannot infer illness, direct another institution, or grant capital funding; a replacement part
requires a request chain, not her judgment. The next shift, and any notice preparation, depends
on what she leaves behind. She depends on the laboratory context, electricity, parts, and a relief
shift that exists on paper.

## Relationship style

Careful and specific with supervisors, whom she treats as people with different pressures rather
than obstacles. Loyal to peers on other shifts, in a practical way — she does not leave a mess
she would not want to inherit. Formal but warm with strangers who arrive with a work order.
Broadly trusting of institutions and procedures, which is exactly where her blind spot sits. At
home she protects routines and schedules that she has no authority over at work.

## Material world

A laminated roster folded into a coat pocket; a steel thermos with a dented base; a hand-kept
logbook she prefers to the display; a damp access badge; spare cabinet seals in a jacket pocket;
an old phone with a cracked screen she has no plans to replace.

## Place signature

Lake wind moving across steel and open water before dawn. Pump vibration coming up through wet
concrete. The service-yard notice board with its layered paper. Winter ice narrowing the railing
route. Intermittent alarms, radio static, and the smell of lake-damp metal.

## Story utility

This viewpoint naturally raises questions about how two valid records can resist one answer,
who pays for a comparison, how a public notice gets assembled from unlike observations, how a
maintenance request travels, and where observation stops and instruction begins. It is useful
wherever evidential responsibility crosses an institutional boundary.

## Non-claims

Ownership of the works is unresolved. No facility arrangement, capacity, sensor coverage,
treatment performance, retrofit dimension, notice content, or health outcome is asserted. Her
log-trusting tendency and her narrow handoffs are character traits, not findings about records.
She is one plausible viewpoint among others, and the facility works without her.

# CHAR-K-02 — Dale Renner

Source role: ROLE-02 — Farm drainage steward
Primary region: Black Swamp Country / Maumee River Commons
Status: K
Provisional name: Dale Renner · Alternates: Corinne Feltz, Merle Ottney

## Core identity

In his fifties, at a life stage with no clean retirement date. He is a small farm
operator who shares drainage responsibility along a reach he does not own end to end, working
under a provisional stewardship agreement rather than a service contract. Home and work occupy
the same place: house, equipment shed, repair yard, field road, ditch, and the gate he walks most
mornings. He lives with his spouse; an adult child works off-farm in another county and a nephew
helps seasonally when school allows. He left formal schooling early and has **very little formal
education and very strong practical competence** — machinery, water, weather, and the politics of
a shared ditch. His economic position is **financially insecure** in the ordinary sense: a narrow
cash buffer, one wet season and one major repair away from real difficulty.

## What he is good at

- Reading a ditch and a gate as well as any instrument reads them — bank scour, debris lines,
  which culvert is settling, which field entrance will hold a truck.
- Machinery repair with what is on the shelf, including making a part out of a different part.
- Judging work windows from weather, soil, and the calendar rather than the forecast alone.
- Knowing which neighbor will trade access, which one wants a favor recorded, and which one to
  avoid asking twice.


## What he routinely gets wrong

- He distrusts written agreements and leans on a remembered understanding, then is genuinely
  aggrieved when the other party reads the paper. It costs him leverage, and he has never quite
  accepted that a record is someone else's memory.
- He underweights downstream conditions he cannot see from his own reach, and can hear a complaint
  as an accusation rather than information.
- He defers an expensive repair hoping for a dry window — sometimes correct, sometimes just the cost
  of not wanting to look at the number.
- He can treat his own margin as the obvious priority, forgetting that he is one party to a shared
  maintenance commitment and not the whole of it.

## Workday

Up at 05:00; water on for coffee while he pulls on boots that live by the door. The night was wet,
so before anything else he walks the field entrance and finds a gate obstructed by debris. He
clears it by hand, compares the sensor reading against the marked board he trusts more, and finds
they disagree enough to matter. He records the condition on paper, delays a crossing he had
planned, and calls his counterpart on the shared drainage arrangement — who is not available until
afternoon. Midday he eats out of a cooler on the truck tailgate, then spends two hours on a worn
gate fitting with a part he had to reshape. A contractor he half-expected does
not come. Late afternoon he walks the reach again, checks a culvert marker, and calls the same
person again. Supper is late. He spends twenty minutes with the
paper calendar on its nail, deciding which of tomorrow's tasks can absorb today's loss.

## Life outside the system

He plays slow-pitch softball in a village league where the median age is well above his, hunts deer
in season because it fills a freezer, and is the person who brings the coffee urn to the township
pancake breakfast. None of
this is a comment on drainage.

## Personal pressure

Practical: the repair reserve against the next wet window, with no way to run both.
Relationship: his spouse wants him to stop being the neighborhood's free maintenance department, and
she is not wrong about the arithmetic.
Internal contradiction: he honors shared maintenance commitments and resents that the shared
calendar never matches his, and he wants to be independent while depending on a shared crew,
neighbor consent, and outside inputs.

## Authority and dependence

He sets his own field-work timing and can request shared maintenance. He cannot command
downstream water or alter a neighbor's structures, and his stewardship agreement gives him
obligations without operational control. The season's work calendar and the counterpart drainage
handoffs depend on him showing up. He depends on a shared crew, access consent, parts, outside
inputs, and the weather, none of which he decides.

## Relationship style

Wary and formal with agencies, generous but keeping-score with neighbors, gruff with strangers and
reliable once he has agreed to something. He is his own supervisor and therefore stubborn with
anyone who tries to supervise him. At home he is affectionate, inattentive to the calendar, and
vulnerable to the accusation that he is never finished.

## Material world

Boots by the door with the mud left on; a canvas tote of spare gate pins and clamps; a paper
calendar on a nail; a thermos whose lid does not match it;
a folded stewardship notice in the glovebox; a marked measuring stick for the ditch.

## Place signature

Ditch water and gravel in spring. Reeds moving in wind while a pump vibrates near the control box.
Hot metal, humid air, and cicadas collecting around the repair yard in July. Diesel, wet field, and
the low sound of water moving where he did not put it.

## Story utility

This viewpoint exposes consent, calendars, and weather as constraints that functioning equipment
does not remove. It is useful for questions about who maintains shared drainage, what an oral
understanding is worth when the other party keeps records, and how different an inspected reach is
from a downstream condition.

## Non-claims

No parcel, crop, yield, nutrient-removal, ecological, or water-quality outcome is asserted, and no
downstream harm or benefit is demonstrated. Black Swamp Country is interpretive; no held Great
Black Swamp geometry, ownership determination, or service territory is used. His informality is a
character limitation, not a claim about how such arrangements should work.

# CHAR-K-03 — Adam Szabo

Source role: ROLE-03 — Materials qualification worker
Primary region: Great Lakes Industrial Belt
Status: K
Provisional name: Adam Szabo · Alternates: Nia Whitcomb, Peter Ainsley

## Core identity

Early establishing-career, around thirty. He works at a bench and process-quality station for a
private industrial employer in reused industrial fabric he commutes to and does not own. He lives
in a small upper flat with his partner, saving with specific intent toward a house, and is in broad
terms moderately secure and not comfortable. His education matters only in one respect: a two-year
technical program plus employer certification taught him sampling, calibration, and how to read a
receiving specification, and the plant taught him everything else.

## What he is good at

- Knowing exactly where comparability ends: which measurement answers which question and which
  one has quietly stopped doing so.
- Labeling, segregating, and storing samples so that a record survives contact with a busy shift.
- Reading a receiving standard against an available characterization and naming the gap.
- Writing a nonconformance record that a reviewer cannot easily wave away.
- Calibration and instrument hygiene — small, dull, and the thing he is best at.

## What he routinely gets wrong

- He **trusts procedure more than his own intuition**, and can hold a lot long past the point where
  a more senior colleague would simply pick up the phone. He is not wrong to be careful; he is
  slow to act on the informal reading that the procedure does not express.
- He mistakes a complete record for a correct decision, including his own.
- He underestimates how a deadline looks from the loader's seat, where storage space is a physical
  problem rather than a scheduling one.
- He overestimates how much the specification actually covers, and can be surprised to find that
  the buyer's standard is silent on the thing being argued about.

## Workday

A 06:15 bus to the district; badge, cabinet, bench. First task is a sample carried up from a tagged
container in the segregated bay, with the calibration sheet checked before the sample is touched.
He compares the characterization to the receiving specification and finds the coverage ends one
step short of the question being asked. His supervisor arrives wanting storage space released; he
places the lot on internal hold, writes the tag, and documents the nonconformance in wording he
knows is slightly more cautious than the situation requires. Lunch is in a break room with a
vending machine and a window onto a service road. The afternoon is storage work and segregation
tags, then a calibration check on a second instrument, then the walk to the gate past the loading
edge with rail vibration coming through the pavement. He signs out at 15:40 and rides a bus home
that stops four times on the way.

## Life outside the system

He plays keyboard in a cover band that gets two paying nights a month and practices badly on
Wednesdays. He runs long distances slowly and without a plan. He and his partner host a monthly
card night with two other couples, which is the main way he keeps friends he made before this job.

## Personal pressure

Practical: the shift premium he would like and the overtime he keeps declining because it collides
with his partner's schedule, plus a storage cost that attaches to every held lot.
Relationship: her family expects them at Sunday dinners that his rotation keeps eating, and he has
run out of creative apologies.
Internal contradiction: he wants to be the person trusted with the call, and he avoids the
informal call because a wrong one would be his. Professional caution versus a real desire to close
something.

## Authority and dependence

He can place a lot on internal hold and document nonconformance. He cannot rewrite a buyer's
standard, certify beyond delegated scope, or issue a permit; disclosure of a lot record belongs to
a separate data owner. Receiving workers and the traceability record depend on what he writes.
He depends on accepted methods, laboratory sign-off, contractual specification, and a supervisor
who tolerates a hold.

## Relationship style

Deferential to supervisors in the vocabulary of compliance and quietly stubborn in what he
actually does. Careful with peers, some of whom experience him as a bottleneck. Polite, slightly
stiff with strangers on other shifts. He respects institutional rules and has recently begun to
notice that rules do not decide things — a suspicion he has not yet said out loud to anyone at
work.

## Material world

A sample jar in a padded carrier; a stack of segregation tags; a printed specification binder with
his own marginal marks; calibration sheets in a plastic sleeve; a steel pen he has used for years;
badge on a lanyard; work shoes with worn outer soles; a lunch container he rinses in a break-room
sink.

## Place signature

Tagged storage beside an old loading bay. Rail vibration through patched pavement. Heat radiating
off pipework into humid air. Fan noise and forklift beeps traveling between concrete bays.
Lake-weathered steel on the walk to the gate.

## Story utility

This viewpoint exposes the distance between a recoverable stream and a qualified input, and
between measurement and decision. It is useful for questions about who owns a standard, who may
disclose a record, why a hold is not a rejection, and how a defensible cautious decision still
produces a cost somewhere else.

## Non-claims

No exact process, throughput, material performance, profitability, market outcome, or deployment
map is asserted, and residual is not treated as automatically recoverable or qualified. The buyer,
laboratory, and data owner remain separate off-matrix participants. His caution is a character
trait, not scientific guidance.

# CHAR-K-04 — Renata Boyd

Source role: ROLE-04 — Resident caregiver and shift worker
Primary region: Glass City Core
Status: K
Provisional name: Renata Boyd · Alternates: Yvonne Larkin, Cass Whitfield

## Core identity

Mid-thirties. She works paid hourly shifts for an employer whose business has nothing to do with
infrastructure, and she runs a household that includes her mother, who has limited mobility, and
her nine-year-old daughter. They live in a retrofitted building in the urban core; her day is a
route between home, a bus stop, her workplace, a school bus stop, and — when it works out — a
staffed cooling room. Her economic position is **financially insecure in the specific way hourly
work makes you insecure**: the pay is regular until it is not, and every hour spent waiting is an
hour not paid. She has very little formal authority of any kind.

## What she is good at

- Detailed practical knowledge of routes, queue rhythms, opening hours, and which driver on which
  run will wait ten seconds.
- Protecting another person's rest and routine — including knowing when her mother wants company and
  when she wants to be left alone.
- Reorganizing care cover at short notice across two or three people who each have their own jobs.
- Holding a household calendar, medication timing, and two shift patterns in her head at once.
- Getting a wrong posted detail corrected, because she knows which office to ask and asks
  pleasantly and repeatedly.

## What she routinely gets wrong

- She delays an uncomfortable request. She would rather absorb one more lost shift than ask her
  manager for a schedule change a second time.
- She **trusts a posted opening to mean an actual opening**, and arranges a day around it before
  checking anything else. Accurate information and usable information are not the same thing.
- She overestimates how much she can absorb — one more wait, one more unpaid hour — and then runs
  short at the worst moment.
- She misreads her manager's incentive, reading reluctance as coldness when the constraint is
  staffing she cannot see.

## Workday

Up at 05:40. Breakfast, medication set out, hair done while the kettle boils. She walks her
daughter to the school bus stop, then takes a bus to a shift that starts at 08:00 and involves a
counter, a queue, and a supervisor who counts the minutes. Mid-shift her mother calls about a
delivery; she cannot leave and says so. Her shift ends at 16:00 with a bus that will not arrive
before 16:25. The plan for the afternoon was a staffed room whose posted opening is 17:00, which
she now knows will not work before her evening obligations. She fetches her mother anyway, and they
wait on a bench where the building's shade ends and the pavement does not, talking about something
unrelated to any of this. Home by 19:30, supper cooked standing up,
laundry started, her daughter's reading done on the sofa, and a notebook of appointments updated
before she sleeps. No crisis occurs; the day simply does not fit.

## Life outside the system

She sings karaoke badly with a cousin on Friday nights when she can get out. She resells secondhand
children's clothing online out of two plastic bins, which is a small income and a genuine interest
in clothes. She keeps houseplants she is quietly proud of and plays cards on the stoop with a
neighbor in warm months.

## Personal pressure

Practical: money against time — the wages lost to a single unavoidable wait are not recoverable
elsewhere in the month.
Relationship: her daughter is old enough to notice that her mother is always arranging something,
and her mother resists being arranged at all.
Internal contradiction: she wants care to remain a relationship rather than a second unpaid job,
and she is the person who always covers, which is partly generosity and partly a habit she has
never tested.

## Authority and dependence

She can choose her own arrangements and request that an error be corrected. She cannot open a room,
direct staffing, or secure a paid schedule change on anyone else's behalf. Her daughter and her
mother depend on her in concrete, daily ways. She depends on staffed access, dependable notices,
paid time, and another person's consent, and on a manager who is not required to be flexible.

## Relationship style

Direct and warm with peers in the same situation. Guarded with managers, polite in
person and blunt at home. Efficient with strangers, and slightly impatient with people who have
time to spare. With institutions she is deferential face to face and frustrated afterwards. With
household members she is protective, loyal, and occasionally sharp when she is tired.

## Material world

A printed hours sheet folded into the bottom of her bag; a refillable water bottle with a scuffed
base; an old phone with a cracked case; a shoulder bag that also carries a spare cardigan for her
mother; a transit card; a plastic lunch container; a small notebook of appointments in two colors.

## Place signature

The shaded side of a street that ends where pavement meets full sun. The doorway of a retrofitted
building with a laminated notice behind glass. A bus stop beside reflective glass that holds heat.
A laundromat's humid air. A stoop in the evening with two people and a deck of cards.

## Story utility

This viewpoint exposes the difference between infrastructure provision, staffed access, and usable
household time. It is useful for questions about what a notice is worth when it is accurate but
ill-timed, what waiting costs in wages, and how care responsibilities move to someone else when an
option is declined.

## Non-claims

No exposure, dose, illness, heat-health, coverage, or household-entitlement outcome is asserted.
Adaptation is not treated as eliminating heat stress. Her employer, building, and the staffing
constraints behind the notice remain separate and largely off-screen. Her difficulty is ordinary
schedule arithmetic, not a claim about any program's adequacy.

# CHAR-K-05 — Hannah Priebe

Source role: ROLE-05 — Field sampling worker
Primary region: Maumee River Commons / Black Swamp Country
Status: K
Provisional name: Hannah Priebe · Alternates: Toby Rennick, Grace Ostrander

## Core identity

**Early-career**, mid-twenties, in her second year on a program field team. She collects samples
under assigned methods and travels from settlement edges out to generalized ditch and wetland
access points. Laboratory work and any release decision sit with offices she has never visited.
She shares a rented duplex with two roommates on different shift patterns, at a pay level that
leaves no cushion and no crisis. Her relevant training is a bachelor's-level environmental program
plus the program's own method qualification; her actual skill with a wetland gate came from
someone showing her once.

## What she is good at

- Method discipline: recording effort, weather, route, and custody in a way that survives later
  scrutiny, even when the day has gone badly.
- Growing local access knowledge — which landowner opens which gate, which track floods, which
  culvert is passable after rain.
- Equipment care: traps cleaned, coolers packed, batteries charged and carried twice.
- Telling a supervisor something unhelpful at the right moment instead of a tidy version later.
- Reading a route's timing from light, wind, and distance rather than the schedule.

## What she routinely gets wrong

- She **trusts field experience more than any dashboard**, and it shades into overvaluing her own
  observation: a thing she saw herself feels more comparable to a previous visit than it actually
  is.
- She underestimates how a caveat reads to someone who needs a number. She writes limitations
  plainly and assumes they will be understood as she means them.
- She overestimates what a repeat visit can fix, and will argue for one before considering what it
  displaces.
- She dislikes being the person who produces an unremarkable, uninterruptible series, and is
  quietly tempted by the interesting result.

## Workday

Loads the vehicle at 05:20, checking the cooler and battery packs against a paper list. Drives out
past the last streetlight to a wetland edge where she checks traps, replaces a battery, labels a
container, and records weather and method by hand while insects move through the reeds. Mid-morning
she meets a landowner at a gate and spends eight minutes being told about last year's water. Lunch
is a sandwich on the tailgate with the cooler lid for a table. At the second site the access track
is wetter than expected, so she logs the deviation from the intended route rather than skipping the
note. One container goes into custody late; the laboratory window is still open. She returns,
finishes the paperwork, writes tomorrow's route on paper because the field tablet is charging, and
drives home in the dark, thinking about what she should have asked the landowner. She stops for fuel
and a bag of ice on the way.

## Life outside the system

She plays recreational volleyball on Tuesdays, mostly for the argument afterwards. She has half of
a shared vegetable plot at a community site, which she joined for the company rather than the
produce. She plays a cooperative online game with a friend who moved two states away, which is how
they still talk.

## Personal pressure

Practical: her rent is likely to rise past what her route mileage and pay will comfortably cover,
and moving farther out would lengthen the commute that eats her field time.
Relationship: her closest friend and roommate is moving out, which is both a money problem and the
end of an arrangement she relied on more than she said.
Internal contradiction: she wants to be trusted enough to report ambiguity rather than produce a
tidy series, and she also wants to be visibly good at her job and be offered the interesting route.

## Authority and dependence

She can mark a sample limitation and request a repeat visit. She cannot diagnose anything, infer
incidence, issue a health order, or repurpose a sample for a question it was not collected to
answer. The interpretability of her samples and the continuity of the next route depend on her
records. She depends on access consent, a cooler, a vehicle, laboratory reception, and an office
she does not control deciding what may be said publicly.

## Relationship style

Eager with supervisors in a way she is starting to correct by asking better questions. Collaborative
and unguarded with peers. Initially awkward with landowners and effective once she remembers their
names. Respectful of institutions, impatient with their timelines, and prone to taking the delay
personally. Easygoing at home with people who do not ask her to organize anything.

## Material world

An insulated cooler with a taped-on label; a pad of paper chain-of-custody forms; gloves and a
spare pair; a canvas bag holding a second battery pack; a mud-stained field notebook; calf-high
boots; a phone in a plastic sleeve; a water jug that is always slightly warm by noon.

## Place signature

Wetland insects in reeds and humid air before the sun is high. Rain drumming on a vehicle roof.
A cooler lid snapping shut on a muddy access track. The hum of a laboratory refrigerator at the
handoff window. Gravel, tire ruts, and a gate that has to be lifted to close.

## Story utility

This viewpoint exposes comparability as work rather than arithmetic. It is useful for questions
about what it takes to compare a detection with a non-detection collected under different effort,
how a method caveat travels from a field notebook toward a public notice, and what the person who
collects a signal can and cannot say about it.

## Non-claims

Detection is not incidence, population burden, transmission, or disease; non-detection does not
establish absence. No pathogen, local disease event, sampling coverage, deployment map, or response
outcome is specified. Her preference for field evidence is a character trait, not a scientific
position, and her route details remain generalized rather than a held geography.

# CHAR-K-06 — Wendell Kasparek

Source role: ROLE-08 — Small repair haulier
Primary region: Great Lakes Industrial Belt / Maumee River Commons
Status: K
Provisional name: Wendell Kasparek · Alternates: Bud Ferraro, Lena Prior

## Core identity

**Later-career**, early sixties, still working because the work is his and stopping is a decision he
keeps not making. He is an owner-driver: he moves repair parts and compatible loads
between farm repair yards and industrial loading edges, paid per booking, with the vehicle as the
main asset in the business. He lives with his wife, who works part-time at a care home, in a small
house with a tomato bed and a very old dog; their adult son lives two hours away and has opinions
about the truck. High school, some trade courses, and thirty years of practical logistics. In
broad terms he is **economically stable but time-poor and thin-margined**: solvent, and one
transmission away from not being.

## What he is good at

- Verifying tags, seals, and loading paperwork against what is actually on the dock, including
  catching a wrong lot before it is on his deck.
- Practical timing: which yard gate is really open at 06:00, which appointment slips by an hour,
  which receiver will be ready before noon.
- Vehicle care and roadside repair — he can limp a load home and still make the second booking.
- Deciding fast whether a booking is a bad deal, and having the nerve to say no out loud.
- Knowing who at each yard actually knows what is happening.

## What he routinely gets wrong

- He **dislikes dealing with institutional systems and avoids them**, which means he leaves the
  paperwork that would protect him until it is a problem, and takes the losses quietly rather
  than filing anything.
- He assumes a yard's rules are stable, and is repeatedly surprised by staff turnover and by a new
  procedure nobody mentioned to drivers.
- He underestimates what his own evenings cost him: the unpaid hour at 19:00 appears in no number he
  looks at.
- He misreads a dispatcher's incentive: a request gets treated as a requirement, so he says yes to
  a changed appointment that costs him the next one.

## Workday

A walk-around with a flashlight at 04:45, tires kicked out of habit and one checked properly. Drive
to a yard, wait at a gate with the engine off to save fuel. Tags verified against the docket, load
secured with the same two straps, chain checked twice. Drive across the district through freight
noise and patched pavement to a loading edge where he waits forty minutes for a receiving office to
produce a signature. He eats a sandwich and drinks coffee from a thermos in the cab. A call arrives
about an afternoon delivery
change that will not fit his next booking; he takes it, then spends ten minutes working out what he
has just given away. On the way back he tightens a fitting with a wrench from the toolbox at the
side of a lot, because a scheduled repair would take two days he does not have. Dockets reconciled
at the kitchen table at 20:00, and he falls asleep over the last one.

## Life outside the system

He restores a small outboard motor that will probably never be used in earnest, fishes for walleye
with his brother-in-law in season, plays euchre on Tuesdays in a neighbor's garage, and keeps a
tomato bed that produces more than two people can eat. His wife is the one who knows where the
insurance paperwork is.

## Personal pressure

Practical: a repair bill against this month's bookings, with the vehicle's age as a standing
threat.
Relationship: his son wants him to sell the truck and stop, and his wife wants him present at
supper, and neither of them is arguing about the same thing.
Internal contradiction: he values being his own man, and the business only works because of other
people's schedules; he refuses to be a free waiting service in principle while absorbing unpaid
hours in practice.

## Authority and dependence

He can accept or refuse his own bookings. He cannot qualify material, bypass yard rules, or promise
anyone access. His customers' parts arrive when he does. He depends on payment terms, permitted
loading access, parts, receiving staff, and a vehicle that keeps running, and on no institution
being interested enough to help.

## Relationship style

Friendly and blunt with other drivers, with whom he shares real information. Careful and slightly
deferential with yard staff he needs. Impatient and evasive with offices and forms, which he treats
as weather. Formal and polite with strangers. Generous with household members and completely
immovable about the truck.

## Material world

A coil-bound docket book with two years of entries; a tire gauge; a thermos with a chipped lid; a
length of chain and two load straps; gloves with the fingers worn through; an old phone on a belt
clip; an envelope of unpaid notices behind the sun visor; a canvas jacket that smells of diesel.

## Place signature

Repair yards and freight noise at the start of the day. A weigh-station light coming on while he
waits. Rail vibration along the loading edge. Cold
morning air at a yard gate with the engine off and the window down.

## Story utility

This viewpoint exposes how a reasonable substitution becomes someone's labor, liability, and lost
booking. It is useful for questions about what actually gates material movement — a booking, a tag,
a receiving signature, a qualification — and what it costs a small operator to be accommodating in
a system he has decided not to argue with.
