# Phase 17D HF-02 — Lake Erie Energy & Security Coast 2075 composition studies

**Corrected low-resolution study accepted for final-production planning · B / L1 · Phase 17D active · S/K; no new C**

This record documents a bounded test of whether the [Phase 17D house style v1.0](../docs/phase_briefs/phase17d_public_visual_style_guide.md) can work at an exposed Lake Erie utility and working-waterfront edge. Human review accepted the corrected low-resolution study and its B / L1 direction for final-production planning. Final HF-02 production has not begun. This report draws on the accepted [HF-02 concept](phase17d_hero_futurescape_concepts.md), [Phase 17D synthesis](../docs/phase_briefs/phase17d_futurist_atlas_synthesis.md), [invention catalog](../docs/phase_briefs/phase17d_glass_basin_invention_catalog.md), and [three-hero retrospective](phase17d_three_hero_visual_retrospective.md). It does not accept a final hero, create assets, authorize publication, change the public site, assign new GBI IDs or create canon. The real Toledo intake crib remains a **separate identifiable reference asset**, never the implied viewpoint, host or surveyed neighbor of this composite terrace.

## Common proposition and visual guardrails

Each option depicts one synthetic, plausible 2075 working-coast moment. A **horizontal lake-edge service terrace** thickens an inherited concrete quay: protected optical comparison/inspection space sits behind weather-exposed screens; a recovery cradle gives a returned **civilian environmental survey / observation craft** a shore-side dry place to stop and be serviced; landward conversion, storage, isolation and service spaces make energy infrastructure physical; a conventional workboat and freight/service access preserve an open system. The parts belong to different operators and review chains. Utility, port, environmental and public-information responsibilities can be suggested by separated gates, staffed handoffs and a bounded notice point, but no sign or uniform proves jurisdiction.

The shared spatial reading is **land/service approach → weather-protected terrace → recovery/instrument/energy interfaces → working quay → open Lake Erie**. Keep a low Great Lakes horizon and an appreciable amount of Lake Erie water. Do not make the terrace a generic glass building set next to water. Use scarred concrete, coated/galvanized steel, corroded joints, dark seals, composite housings, mineral/weather streaks, fouling, dock hardware and workboats. This is a freshwater coast: avoid visual shorthand that depends on a saltwater harbor. Functional glass is protected, sacrificial or replaceable; most machinery remains opaque and serviceable. No glowing reactor, power beam, fortress, weapon, military crew, generic sci-fi skyline, exact energy technology or hidden treatment result.

The image should show **one clear routine maintenance state**, not a crane-hung object. A craft may return, settle into a dry cradle and be handed to people; a sensor package can move on a low sled; a technician can exchange a weather screen at reachable height. Weather can stop work. An empty berth or isolated compartment gives failure a place in the architecture. Conventional port and utility work continues alongside this future system, and energy/observation does not imply a closed or continuously available network. At 390 px a viewer should see coast, horizontal terrace and one worker/recovery cue before any text.

## Three weather/light treatments

| Treatment | What it reveals | Risks and control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **L1 — clear, windy lake day** | Sharp daylight, low horizon, whitecaps, flags/straps under tension, and hard highlights distinguish exposed quay from deep protected galleries. A crew can work in a service lee while open water remains active. | Avoid resort-blue leisure water, dazzling mirrored pavement or a postcard sunset. Wind is shown by surface texture and secured equipment, not a forecast of a specific event. |
| **L2 — lake haze / morning fog** | Receding freight and water soften while nearby corroded hardware, sacrificial screens and protected optical bench remain legible. Exposure gives the terrace a reason for depth. | Haze must not conceal the land-to-lake relationship or become suspense/security theater. No assumed treatment, visibility guarantee or exact weather frequency. |
| **L3 — hard winter light / cold clear** | Low cold sun, long shadows and secured gear make access and weather delay concrete; the horizon is crisp and the water remains Lake Erie. | Do not fabricate simultaneous summer crops and ice, assert future ice cover, or use frost as a generic apocalypse filter. Dry cold surfaces or localized icy residue are enough for a composition test. |

These are alternatives for visual study, not climate forecasts. The recommended first low-resolution round uses **L1** because it shows the water, terrace layering and service actions together while extending the house palette beyond HF-01's amber break, HF-03's broken cloud and HF-04's dusty interior.

## A — recovery cradle dominant

**Visual proposition:** the returning observation craft and its grounded dry cradle are the emotional center. The terrace wraps around this task, and open water explains why the cradle is there. The image must make recovery a human handoff, not a spectacular lift.

- **Camera height:** At standing crew height beside a recessed dry cradle, close enough to see wet hardware and a person reaching the returned craft; not a dramatic low heroic angle.
- **Camera direction:** Look from the landward lip of the cradle diagonally through its mouth to the quay and open lake. The protected terrace runs laterally behind the crew.
- **Foreground:** A compact civilian environmental survey craft is already settled on low rollers or chocks in a dry berth. A secured line, wet deck, drainable work surface and a worker's tool make its stopped state clear; avoid torpedo-like or military visual language.
- **Midground:** A second crew member moves a sensor case onto a service sled. Protected optical comparison space, a sacrificial outer screen and an empty adjacent berth are visible in the terrace wall.
- **Background:** Conventional workboat, cargo/service movement and low working-waterfront fragments recede without creating a specific surveyed port panorama. The real intake crib is omitted.
- **Lake horizon:** Seen beyond the cradle mouth, above the working quay; wide enough to establish exposed water, not reduced to a bright strip behind a machine.
- **Primary silhouette:** A broad **U-shaped dry recovery cut** interrupts an otherwise low horizontal terrace. The void and its protective overhang, rather than a suspended craft, define the future form.
- **Crew activity:** Two people secure, disconnect and inspect the returned unit; a separate authorized operator attends the service boundary. People do not pose in a line around the craft.
- **Recovery / inspection action:** Tether released only after the craft settles; sensor package goes to a protected comparison bench. This depicts a possible handoff, not an operating or safety procedure.
- **Energy-interface visibility:** A landward opaque conversion/storage/isolation bay appears as a separate service mass with accessible doors, vents and protected connections. No device type or grid output is identified.
- **Public / restricted boundary:** A small staffed threshold and public-information face sit landward of the berth. The public does not enter the wet work zone; a fence alone does not stand in for governance.
- **Conventional port / utility context:** Workboat, quay bollards, cable service, rolling cases and ordinary utility truck keep the coast an open working system.
- **Weather option:** **L2 lake haze** would prioritize near-field repair and screen wear while leaving enough lake/horizon visible. A clear-light control would be needed if haze hides the terrace.
- **Material hierarchy:** Wet dark rubber and fouled instrument housing first; scarred concrete cradle, coated steel and replaceable cloudy screen next; opaque energy room and pale lake behind.
- **Corrosion / fouling cue:** Mineral streaks below railings, oxidized fixings, algae/fouling on recovered surfaces, and a scratched sacrificial panel indicate freshwater exposure without implying system failure rate.
- **Maintenance / failure cue:** The craft is stopped, one adjacent berth is isolated, and a technician checks a fouled instrument; no continuous observation or unattended operation is implied.
- **At 390 px:** The U-cut, dark settled craft, crew and water mouth should survive. If the cradle becomes a generic dock basin and energy separation disappears, the option fails.
- **What remains K:** Exact craft/cradle geometry, sensor case, institutional threshold, equipment relation, operating sequence and siting are invented; no crib attachment or continuity claim.
- **Intentionally omitted:** Suspended craft, crane hook, soldiers, glowing energy source, detailed assay display, named port building, treatment outlet and futuristic skyline.
- **Primary failure mode:** A compelling boat-recovery poster whose terrace, energy compartments and public boundary retreat into background detail. It may repeat the first two heroes' single-object focus even without a crane.

## B — service terrace / coast section dominant

**Visual proposition:** the whole horizontal land-to-lake section is the hero. Recessed recovery, protected inspection and isolated energy service form a recognizable working coastline, while a crew performs an ordinary screen exchange and a returned craft rests below.

- **Camera height:** Slightly raised from a service approach, about a person above the quay level; enough to see terrace depth and the water-side cut, never a survey aerial.
- **Camera direction:** Look obliquely along the terrace from land on the left toward the working quay and broad lake on the right. The perspective should separate bands rather than flatten them into a single facade.
- **Foreground:** Scarred concrete approach, utility/service threshold, secured cases and a technician replacing one cloudy sacrificial weather screen at reachable height. The old quay joint stays visible.
- **Midground:** Alternating protected optical bays, opaque conversion/storage/isolation rooms, an **open/isolated service gap**, and a dry cradle containing a stopped, secured civilian environmental survey craft. A small crew handles a sensor on a low sled.
- **Background:** Conventional workboat and bounded port/freight activity remain beyond a separate operational line. Avoid skyline drama and any apparent line of sight to the real crib.
- **Lake horizon:** A long, low band of open Lake Erie occupies the right and upper distance. It is essential negative space and an exposure source, not decorative blue behind the building.
- **Primary silhouette:** A **low stepped terrace with deep horizontal recesses and one U-shaped recovery slot**. Protected galleries sit behind a replaceable weather layer; the quay remains exposed below.
- **Crew activity:** One technician changes a screen, two crew members secure a returned civilian environmental survey craft, and ordinary port workers continue farther away. The tasks are dispersed rather than staged around a hanging object.
- **Recovery / inspection action:** A small survey craft is already grounded in the dry slot; a retrieved sensor/instrument case moves to a protected comparison and calibration bench. The open path and waiting crew matter more than the craft's design.
- **Energy-interface visibility:** Opaque landward rooms have distinct service doors, protected electrical connection, vent/thermal-service space and an isolation gap. Do not depict a named generation technology, intake/discharge or performance.
- **Public / restricted boundary:** A staffed access door and separate public notice/viewing edge are visible on the landward approach. No fortress wall or checkpoint dominates the horizon.
- **Conventional port / utility context:** Older bollards, patched rail, workboat, freight/service vehicle and utility connections give the speculative terrace a working neighbor and external dependencies.
- **Weather option:** **L1 clear, windy lake day.** Sharp daylight and whitecaps expose the water/protected-space contrast; wind-pressed straps and screened work reveal why service is recessed.
- **Material hierarchy:** Weathered concrete and lake water lead; coated/galvanized steel, rubber seals, matte opaque enclosures and replaceable optical screens define the new layer. Functional glass is selective.
- **Corrosion / fouling cue:** Streaked fasteners, cloudy outer screens, fouled cradle rollers and patched coating are concentrated at water/exposure interfaces, not scattered as decorative rust.
- **Maintenance / failure cue:** The exchanged screen and isolated gap interrupt the facade; a secured civilian survey craft waits for comparison. No graphic claims that the system recovered or observed continuously.
- **At 390 px:** The stepped horizontal terrace, U-cut, open water and one visible worker/screen action must remain separable. Tiny institutional tags can disappear; the public/restricted split is clarified by the inset.
- **What remains K:** Terrace geometry, energy-room grouping, recovery operation, workers, access zoning, notices and all co-location are speculative; no real shoreline parcel, sightline or facility is implied.
- **Intentionally omitted:** Crib in the hero, big crane, hovering craft, exact energy device, beam, military imagery, glass curtain wall, quantified data overlays and a treatment outfall.
- **Primary failure mode:** A generic futuristic waterfront campus if the long service section becomes one smooth building. Recesses, exposure, old quay, working boat and distinct service actions are the correction criteria.

## C — lake / human operations dominant

**Visual proposition:** the viewer enters through weather and ordinary crew work. Open water is the main mass; the terrace is a secondary but unmistakably transformed working edge. This option tests whether 2075 survives a more landscape-led image.

- **Camera height:** Near a standing crew member's eye at quay level. The camera is beside tools and ropes rather than above the entire site.
- **Camera direction:** Look outward across a quay corner toward open lake, with the terrace receding along one side; this differs from A's cradle mouth and B's raised sectional oblique.
- **Foreground:** Two crew members secure a returned craft at a low slip and slide a weathered sensor case onto a trolley. Ice-safe or cold-weather gear may appear only with the chosen winter condition.
- **Midground:** The quay edge, conventional workboat and screened gallery reveal a working port; an open isolated maintenance bay and a protected comparison window show the future infrastructure.
- **Background:** Broad, low lake horizon, distant weather and limited port silhouettes. The scene cannot imply a surveyed view from or toward the Toledo crib.
- **Lake horizon:** The dominant visual line. It should establish exposure and scale, with enough detail in whitecaps or cold light to remain recognizable as working water.
- **Primary silhouette:** Low crew/boat forms against the lake, with the terrace's stepped service ribs at one edge. The architecture must still change the coastline's section.
- **Crew activity:** Workers decide to secure equipment and delay redeployment; a landward technician checks a screen or comparison station. A public viewer stays behind a clear access boundary.
- **Recovery / inspection action:** The returned instrument is handed off and held for comparison. The photo-like moment does not show successful measurement, an environmental result or a guaranteed next deployment.
- **Energy-interface visibility:** A partly visible opaque enclosure, compartment joint and protected service conduit at the landward end. If it reads as anonymous background plant, the image fails the HF-02 brief.
- **Public / restricted boundary:** A quiet rail/door transition and staffed notice point, not soldiers, armed patrols or theatrical surveillance.
- **Conventional port / utility context:** Workboat, bollards, worn rail, utility vehicle and ordinary cargo activity remain legible as inherited use.
- **Weather option:** **L3 hard winter light / cold clear.** Secured idle gear and cold surfaces explain delay; no assumed seasonal ice cover, simultaneous summer foliage or extreme-weather forecast.
- **Material hierarchy:** Open water and cold sky lead; rough quay concrete, dark rubber, coated metal, fogged screens and insulated opaque enclosure carry the service narrative.
- **Corrosion / fouling cue:** Stained fasteners, pitted brackets and fouled instrument case are inspectable near the crew, not atmospheric grime over everything.
- **Maintenance / failure cue:** Secured inactive craft, held sensor case and one isolated gallery panel show an interrupted workday. The personnel can decide to wait.
- **At 390 px:** Water, crew and a single service threshold should survive. The terrace must not shrink to an undecipherable shed edge.
- **What remains K:** Weather moment, coast arrangement, workers, instrument, access threshold, energy interface and operating choice are invented; no continuous autonomous coverage follows.
- **Intentionally omitted:** Suspended recovery object, heroic launch, military hardware, glowing data, named energy plant, crib viewpoint and precise ice/wave conditions.
- **Primary failure mode:** A convincing present-day port weather photograph with futuristic siding too small to matter. The architecture may fail the convergence test despite strong human and lake identity.

## Composition comparison and human decision

| Review gate | A — cradle | B — terrace section | C — lake/crew |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| New silhouette beside HF-01/03/04 | Strong U-cut but still one-object centered. | Strongest horizontal *coastal section* with multiple distinct functions. | Strong horizon; weakest architectural read. |
| Recovery and ordinary labor | Clearest close return/inspection. | Legible return plus screen service without one theatrical focal event. | Strongest crew/weather, return may look like conventional boating. |
| Energy and authority | Hard to keep visible behind cradle. | Separated landward rooms and public/work threshold can be shown spatially. | Likely reduced to caption. |
| 390 px risk | Generic dock or dark boat. | Generic waterfront campus if recesses and gap smooth out. | 2075 disappears behind lake and people. |

**Accepted for final-production planning: B — Service Terrace / Coast Section Dominant, with L1 — Clear, Windy Lake Day.** Human review accepted the corrected low-resolution study; no further composition exploration is requested before final-production planning. The horizontal, weather-exposed, compartmented coast reads as the hero without requiring an old-brick frame or suspended object. The accepted frame keeps the lake substantial while making energy, observation, recovery, access and outside port work spatially separable. This is acceptance of the composition direction only, not of a final HF-02 hero or any final asset.

The accepted **maintenance and human-work language** is: a technician replaces a cloudy sacrificial weather screen at reachable height; a returned civilian environmental survey / observation craft is secured in a dry recovery cradle that reads as a maintenance interface; another worker moves a sensor/instrument case toward comparison and calibration; and ordinary utility/quay work remains visible. Humans remain responsible for recovery, servicing, calibration and access. No crane, dangling craft or heroic rescue is needed. These are visual propositions, not safe operating instructions or evidence of sensing quality.

## Optional Lake Erie ecological / seasonal cues

These are accepted **optional** visual cues, not required motifs. Zebra mussels / dreissenid fouling may appear on lower quay surfaces, fenders, intake or recovery hardware, submerged or splash-zone structures, or recovered equipment to show a maintenance burden. Do not imply exact abundance, distribution, fouling rates, successful control, or colonization of every surface.

Mayflies may appear subtly when seasonally appropriate—for example, a few insects near lights or equipment, or small accumulations on horizontal surfaces. They are not a permanent Lake Erie signature, need not appear in every season, and should not dominate unless a future scene specifically concerns an emergence. Ecological organisms may affect infrastructure, maintenance, visibility and human work; they are not decorative environmental shorthand.
