# FT-01 Water at the Intake

Epistemic status: S — scenario-development material; not canon.

## Place and scale

The macroregion is the Glass City Core / Lake Erie Energy & Security Coast interface. The scale is a shoreline utility facility: intake and treatment rooms, service routes, utility cabinets, and the working-waterfront edge between river, lake, and city.

## 2026 anchor

E: The accepted Phase 1 water system, Phase 14 systems/dependency architecture, and Phase 16B treatment, energy, and coordination stress stages establish the basis. The current physical Toledo Water Works intake crib is a verified asset distinct from nearby monitoring and legacy dataset entities. These records constrain the translation; they do not describe a 2075 installation.

## 2050 transition

The existing water works gains selected probes, sample points, communications links, load-shifting routines, and manual fallback. New cabinets sit beside older panels. A public notice may combine several records while an operator still relies on a binder and a direct call. Additions arrive through work orders, access windows, and negotiated responsibility rather than clean replacement.

## 2075 lived condition

The mature facility is dense with accumulated layers: weathered concrete, steel railings, pipes, pumps, treatment equipment, sealed cabinets, sample storage, and new observation interfaces. River, lake, intake, treatment, energy, and weather readings remain related but non-identical. A decision-support service flags a condition; it does not operate the system. An operator checks time stamps against a sample or maintenance record and contacts another authority before changing a routine. Some readings are late or difficult to compare.

The facility remains connected to electricity, replacement parts, laboratories, communications, regulators, and public communication. A battery or alternate procedure can support a task without making the system closed. Access is divided between restricted service space, shoreline approaches, and public notices.

## A normal operating day

An operator reviews overnight readings, marks a probe for cleaning, and compares a lake observation with the treatment log. A technician checks a cabinet and calls for a cable replacement. The operator asks whether another sample is needed, prepares a conditional notice, and leaves a paper procedure beside the display.

## Artifacts and material culture

- sealed sensor cabinets and tagged sample coolers
- steel railings, ladders, and weathered concrete
- signed work orders and a manual logbook
- access cards and a service-yard notice board
- pump gauges beside a digital condition display
- spare cables, batteries, and cabinet seals
- public notices separating observation from instruction

## Maintenance and repair

Probes require cleaning and calibration. Cables, cabinet seals, batteries, pump components, and access hardware are replaced on different schedules. Ice, corrosion, wet concrete, and restricted access make inspection seasonal. Staff retain paper forms, local gauges, and manual sampling. A technician must decide whether a disagreement is instrument drift, a timing mismatch, or a condition that warrants another observation.

## Failure / stress expression

- Sensor drift or non-comparable time stamps delay coordination.
- Winter ice, lake weather, or a restricted access window delays inspection.
- A replacement cable, battery, or pump component is unavailable when a work order reaches the site.
- Manual fallback is required when communications or advisory software is unavailable.

These expressions show dependency and friction; they do not make treatment failure, exposure, dose, or illness automatic consequences.

## Governance / institutional interface

Who observes: utility operators, sampling staff, environmental observers, and weather services. Who decides: the responsible utility and public authorities within distinct mandates. Who maintains: utility crews and contractors. Who owns: relevant water and facility assets; future ownership is unresolved. Who regulates: applicable environmental, water, safety, and public authorities. Who communicates: utility and public-information offices. AI recommendation remains advisory; observation is not control.

## Sensory environment

Lake wind moves across steel and open water. Pumps vibrate through wet concrete. Winter ice narrows access. An intermittent alarm, radio static, and damp metal accompany the work.

## Regime variation

### A — Coordinated Technological Adaptation

Shared data conventions, cross-agency review, auditable advisory tools, and funded maintenance make comparison and manual fallback ordinary. Authority remains with human institutions.

### B — Uneven Networked Modernization

Some rooms use compatible records while others depend on adapters, phone calls, and paper logs. The operator works across mixed access, staffing, and maintenance arrangements.

### C — High Capability / High Friction Basin

Selected facilities have advanced observation while contracts, legitimacy, cyber procedures, or maintenance authority remain contested. A flagged condition can still wait for a person, permit, or access window.

## Persistence / modification / addition

PERSISTS — water works, intake/treatment chain, utility rooms, service access, sampling, and institutional review.

MODIFIED — cabinets, sampling points, communications, work orders, energy coordination, and public notices.

ADDED — advisory comparison, redundant records, selected distributed-operation interfaces, and more visible manual fallback.

## Uncertainty / non-claims

The repository does not support an exact facility arrangement, capacity, treatment performance, sensor coverage, adoption rate, service territory, retrofit dimension, cost, or health outcome. Denser observation is not shown to prevent contamination, exposure, dose, illness, or failure. The coordination pattern remains S material, not canon.

# FT-02 Farm Drainage as Working Landscape

Epistemic status: S — scenario-development material; not canon.

## Place and scale

The macroregion is the Maumee River Commons / Black Swamp Country interface. The scale runs from farm block and drainage gate to field road, repair yard, wetland edge, and downstream landscape. Black Swamp Country is interpretive; held geometry stays excluded.

## 2026 anchor

E: Accepted Phase 1 hydrography/routing distinctions, Phase 6 ecological layers, Phase 8A/8B/8C nutrient/control layers, and climate/energy interfaces provide the basis. They support field, ditch, river, wetland, observation, and stewardship relationships, not a future parcel or universal design.

## 2050 transition

Drainage controls, wetland elements, water storage, sensors, selected distributed energy, and repair access appear in partial combinations. Field agriculture remains primary. One reach may have a controlled gate while another relies on a manual board and seasonal crew. New equipment must fit old culverts, tracks, pumps, and labor calendars.

## 2075 lived condition

The farm is a maintained composite rather than a technology display. Productive field blocks sit beside ditches, controls, vegetated margins, perennial strips, wetland cells, windbreaks, service roads, and a repair yard. A greenhouse can complement field production without replacing it. A solar canopy may shade a work area while the farm remains tied to outside electricity, fuel, seed, machinery, markets, drainage coordination, and downstream water.

Water levels are not simply commanded from a screen. A sensor reports a condition; a farmer or technician checks the ditch, gate, weather, and access road before a timing change. The landscape contains old timber, concrete, steel, and packed gravel beside weathered composites, cables, controllers, tanks, and tagged parts. The future is visible in maintenance paths as much as planted edges.

## A normal operating day

A farmer checks the field entrance after a wet night and finds a gate obstructed by debris. A technician compares the sensor with a marked board and clears it by hand. The farmer records the condition, delays a crossing, and contacts the drainage operator. A maintenance worker later replaces a sensor cover.

## Artifacts and material culture

- concrete or steel drainage control boxes
- ditch markers, hand tools, and gate boards
- tanks, filters, pumps, and spare hose
- wetland-edge stakes and stewardship notices
- weathered composite panels and timber windbreaks
- solar canopy over a repair or loading area
- muddy service roads and culvert markers
- replacement cables and battery packs

## Maintenance and repair

Gates clog, banks erode, culverts settle, and reeds obscure access. Sensors need cleaning, calibration, batteries, and comparison with a physical water mark. Pumps, filters, panels, and control-box seals require replacement. Ice and washouts damage access. Human judgment remains necessary when field access, drainage timing, habitat, and equipment movement compete.

## Failure / stress expression

- A clogged gate or settled culvert delays both field access and water management.
- A sensor reports late or disagrees with a manual observation.
- Heat, runoff, or energy timing creates a conflict among irrigation, drainage, habitat, and equipment work.
- A repair waits for a part, a shared crew, or safe access after a washout.

A dependency is visible without implying a crop, ecological, or water-quality outcome.

## Governance / institutional interface

Who observes: farmers, drainage operators, technicians, ecological monitors, and stewardship participants. Who decides: land operators for field routines and drainage or stewardship authorities for shared interfaces. Who maintains: farmers, drainage crews, contractors, and conservation staff. Who owns: land-specific and shared structures are unresolved here. Who regulates: applicable water, agricultural, environmental, and drainage authorities. Who communicates: operators, coordination bodies, and stewardship programs. Data informs a decision without becoming automatic control.

## Sensory environment

Spring water lies over frozen margins. Tires press into wet gravel. Reeds move in wind while pumps vibrate near a control box. In summer, hot metal, humid air, and cicadas collect around the repair yard.

## Regime variation

### A — Coordinated Technological Adaptation

Shared maintenance calendars, compatible records, stewardship agreements, and field support connect drainage and habitat work without removing local judgment. Manual inspection remains routine.

### B — Uneven Networked Modernization

Some farms use common interfaces while others bridge old gates, paper records, and newer sensors. Access, repair, and data sharing are negotiated across fields and drainage reaches.

### C — High Capability / High Friction Basin

Selected operations have sophisticated sensing while land access, labor, funding, or downstream legitimacy remains contested. Equipment can display a condition that no institution is ready to act on.

## Persistence / modification / addition

PERSISTS — field agriculture, ditches, roads, pumps, gates, repair yards, external inputs, and downstream connections.

MODIFIED — drainage timing, water storage, monitoring, service access, and vegetated or wetland edges.

ADDED — selected sensors, control interfaces, stewardship records, distributed-energy equipment, and replacement modules.

## Uncertainty / non-claims

The repository does not support an exact parcel, crop or yield, nutrient-removal performance, ecological outcome, adoption path, labor arrangement, cost, service territory, or farm-wide automation claim. No held Great Black Swamp geometry or health outcome is asserted. This remains S material, not canon.

# FT-03 Connected, Not Closed

Epistemic status: S — scenario-development material; not canon.

## Place and scale

The macroregion is the Great Lakes Industrial Belt / Glass City Core interface. The scale is a mixed industrial district: building, process yard, service road, loading edge, utility room, and institutional boundary where material, heat, water, freight, labor, and data meet.

## 2026 anchor

E: Accepted Phase 5 freight, industry, and material interfaces; the Phase 14 dependency crosswalk; and Phase 15 materials, manufacturing, energy, and data interfaces establish the basis. Phase 16B CAND-003 adds qualitative stress logic. The Phase 17A Industrial Exchange prototype is only a prior S/K precedent, not E evidence.

## 2050 transition

Selected thermal, water-handling, qualified-material, repair, and data interfaces become maintainable in specific facilities or agreements. Each brings sampling, contracts, standards, access rules, liability, and replacement work. A residual is characterized and treated before it is called a qualified input. Old loading areas and service roads remain beneath new pipes, cabinets, storage, and quality stations.

## 2075 lived condition

The district looks accumulated: old concrete bays, glass and steel shells, timber repairs, patched pavement, rust, weathered composites, pipework, tagged bins, heat exchangers, treatment cells, and data markers. A segregated stream waits in a marked bay while a quality worker checks a sample. Heat moves toward another building only when temperature, distance, timing, ownership, maintenance, and contract align. Water and material exchanges remain visible at their gates, with a residual outlet beyond the internal loop.

The district is more legible, not closed. It still depends on feedstock, electricity, freight, laboratories, workers, replacement parts, markets, discharge controls, and regulators. A qualified input can wait because a buyer, standard, or repair window is missing. Traceability can show provenance while a queue, disputed sample, or manual inspection remains.

## A normal operating day

An operator checks a tagged container and carries a sample to a qualification bench. A technician finds that the receiving process is at a different temperature and places the stream in storage. A dispatcher reschedules around a repair zone. An inspector asks who owns the residual while a supervisor signs a valve work order and records the external input still required.

## Artifacts and material culture

- tagged bins separating residual, recoverable, and qualified streams
- sample jars, assay benches, and calibration sheets
- heat exchangers, valves, liners, and treatment tanks
- patched steel, concrete service yards, and loading barriers
- storage racks for spare cable, seals, and pipe sections
- visible discharge or residual handling points


## Maintenance and repair

Sensors foul and need cleaning or recalibration. Liners, valves, cables, pumps, thermal components, and storage surfaces wear on different schedules. Forklifts and trucks work around repair zones. A quality worker decides whether an irregular sample reflects material, instrument, or process, while a maintenance lead coordinates access with production and inspection.

## Failure / stress expression

- A residual remains unqualified.
- Temperature, timing, or water-quality requirements do not match at an interface.
- Freight delay or a missing replacement part leaves material in storage.
- A treatment, data, or contract handoff pauses while ownership or liability is clarified.

These are open-system friction, not proof of circular failure or continuous operation.

## Governance / institutional interface

Who observes: operators, quality staff, laboratories, maintenance crews, freight workers, and environmental monitors. Who decides: facility owners and operators for process acceptance, with other authorities within their mandates. Who maintains: plant crews, contractors, and service providers. Who owns: facility and material ownership varies by contract and is unresolved. Who regulates: environmental, safety, freight, and industrial authorities. Who communicates: facility managers, regulators, and public offices. Data supports provenance; it does not replace a permit, contract, or accountable decision.

## Sensory environment

Fan noise and forklift beeps travel between concrete bays. Hot pipework radiates into humid air. Rail vibration reaches tagged storage. Lake-weathered steel carries dampness; welding flash lights a wall.

## Regime variation

### A — Coordinated Technological Adaptation

Shared standards, auditable records, planned maintenance, and cross-facility agreements make qualification and substitution visible. External inputs and outputs remain accounted for.

### B — Uneven Networked Modernization

Some facilities exchange compatible records while others use translators, paper tags, and negotiated contracts. Streams move through adapters, with maintenance and market access differing by interface.

### C — High Capability / High Friction Basin

Selected plants have advanced characterization while ownership, liability, access, or regulatory legitimacy remains contested. A legible stream can still wait for authorization or an external buyer.

## Persistence / modification / addition

PERSISTS — industrial buildings, yards, roads, freight, external inputs, outputs, residuals, labor, and regulation.

MODIFIED — plant edges, thermal and water interfaces, material handling, access, repair routines, and data exchange.

ADDED — qualification stations, recovery or treatment cells, storage, traceability, replacement spaces, and negotiated interface contracts.

## Uncertainty / non-claims

The repository does not support an exact facility, process, capacity, throughput, material performance, adoption rate, market outcome, or deployment map. Residual is not automatically a resource, and a qualified input is not guaranteed to be used. This S material is not canon.

# FT-04 Heat Between Buildings

Epistemic status: S — scenario-development material; not canon.

## Place and scale

The macroregion is the Glass City Core. The scale is block and district: buildings, streets, roofs, tree pits, cooling rooms, service corridors, drains, and short routes between them.

## 2026 anchor

E: Accepted Phase 9 climate/hazard layers, Phase 11 settlement/mobility context, Phase 15 energy, sensing, automation, and governance boundaries, and Phase 16B CAND-001 provide the basis. They do not establish a neighborhood heat-health forecast or outage claim.

## 2050 transition

Some blocks gain shade, tree pits, water stations, cooling rooms, heat notices, and load-shifting routines. Other blocks retain exposed concrete, dark roofs, glass façades, aging pipes, and limited shade. Managers, utilities, clinics, employers, and residents negotiate opening hours and maintenance. A new canopy may stand over patched pavement while an old service corridor remains hot.

## 2075 lived condition

A neighborhood is a layered city of brick, glass, concrete, timber, pavement, old pipes, planted edges, shade membranes, roof equipment, and cooling infrastructure. One street can function as a cooler public room while the next remains hard, bright, and windless. A damp tree pit or maintained water point is infrastructure. A cooling room depends on staff, power, water, access, notices, and a building that can open.

Sensors make conditions more visible, but do not cool a wall or staff a room. An advisory system can suggest a load change or schedule; a building manager, utility operator, employer, or public office decides what can be powered, staffed, cleaned, and communicated. Residents still plan around shade, routes, access, and work timing.

## A normal operating day

A maintenance worker checks a tree pit, clears a storm drain after a summer burst, and finds a pump needing service. A building manager compares the heat board with room conditions and calls a utility operator. A resident checks room hours. Staff move a notice when the room opens late, while a technician records a rooftop access problem.

## Artifacts and material culture

- public heat boards and printed notices
- tree-pit guards, soil or planting maintenance tags
- rooftop fans, filters, and cooling-room access signs
- sensor poles and portable temperature instruments
- work orders, hydration supplies, and service ladders
- old brick, glass façades, concrete walls, and new brackets

## Maintenance and repair

Trees, planted edges, shade membranes, roofs, drains, pumps, filters, water stations, and cooling equipment need cleaning, watering, calibration, replacement, or staffing. Heat exposes blocked drains, broken latches, empty tanks, and rooms that cannot open. Manual readings and paper notices remain useful when tools do not match field conditions.

## Failure / stress expression

- A cooling room opens late because staffing, access, or equipment is not ready.
- A shaded route ends at a block with exposed pavement and no maintained water point.
- A pump, drain, or feeder reaches a timing conflict during heat and sudden rain.
- A sensor reports a condition without changing the physical street or building.

These expressions do not assign heat-health outcomes or imply that adaptation removes exposure.

## Governance / institutional interface

Who observes: building managers, residents, maintenance crews, utilities, clinics, and public-information staff. Who decides: asset owners and responsible public or utility authorities. Who maintains: building staff, municipal or contracted crews, utility workers, and service providers. Who owns: buildings, public-space features, and equipment have distinct owners; future arrangements are unresolved. Who regulates: applicable building, water, energy, labor, and public authorities. Who communicates: managers, utilities, employers, clinics, and public offices. Advisory information is not authority or control.

## Sensory environment

Humid summer heat hangs between buildings. Cicadas continue behind rooftop fan noise. Bright glass gives way to shaded timber or patched pavement. A storm sends water into a drain while hot concrete and wet leaves remain close to the street.

## Regime variation

### A — Coordinated Technological Adaptation

Cooling access, maintenance calendars, utility coordination, and public notices use auditable routines. Responsibility for opening, cleaning, and communicating is explicit.

### B — Uneven Networked Modernization

Some buildings share heat information and load routines while others rely on local notices, portable equipment, and manual checks. Shade, cooling access, and repair staffing differ by block.

### C — High Capability / High Friction Basin

Selected buildings use advanced sensing or cooling coordination while access, staffing, affordability, or trust remains contested. A recommendation may arrive before a room or crew is available.

## Persistence / modification / addition

ADDED — selective sensing, advisory load coordination, planted edges, cooling equipment, and block-scale heat boards.

## Uncertainty / non-claims

No block is fully protected, and adaptation is not assumed to remove heat stress. This S material is not canon.

# FT-10 Signal, Sampling, and Stewardship

Epistemic status: S — scenario-development material; not canon.

## Place and scale

The macroregion is the Black Swamp Country / Maumee River Commons interface. The scale runs from wetland edge, ditch, settlement, and field vehicle to laboratory, public-health or environmental office, and the public notice channel connecting them. Black Swamp Country is interpretive only; the Great Black Swamp hold remains in force.

## 2026 anchor

E: Accepted Phase 12A/B/C vector ecology layers and Phase 13A/B/C infectious-disease layers preserve the distinctions among vector presence, abundance, pathogen detection, human-vector contact, infection, disease, surveillance, and incidence. Phase 15A molecular-monitoring and data-governance interfaces and Phase 16B CAND-004 provide the observation and coordination basis. No future incidence or transmission model is added.

## 2050 transition

Selected programs add sampling routes, laboratory handoffs, diagnostic tools, aggregate reporting, privacy-preserving access, manual fallback, public communication, and stewardship. Methods do not become identical because records can be exchanged. Some field teams use connected equipment; others carry paper chain-of-custody forms.

## 2075 lived condition

Wetlands, ditches, settlement edges, and institutions are connected by a visible chain of custody. Traps sit beside coolers, battery packs, gloves, calibration sheets, and vehicle racks. A laboratory qualifies a result under a documented identity. A data steward controls aggregate access, while a public-health or environmental office decides what can be communicated and with what caveat.

A dashboard can display a signal without establishing transmission. A detection or diagnostic result does not by itself establish transmission, population incidence, disease burden, or clinical illness. Presence is not transmission; a surveillance signal is not incidence; infection is not clinical disease. Uncertainty is ordinary stewardship: a sample is delayed, methods differ, privacy limits release, or a field observation needs another visit. Digital provenance sits beside handwritten forms and local interpretation.

## A normal operating day

A field sampler checks a trap, replaces a battery, labels a container, and records weather and method. A laboratory technician checks chain of custody and the method record. A data steward prepares an aggregate view. A health or environmental office reviews the wording before a notice is issued; the field team keeps the next route on paper.

## Artifacts and material culture

- paper chain-of-custody forms and calibration logs
- laboratory freezers, labels, and access-controlled records

## Maintenance and repair

Traps, sensors, batteries, coolers, freezers, labels, and vehicles require cleaning, calibration, charging, and replacement. Routes must be revisited when access is blocked or a method changes. Laboratory equipment has a separate cycle. A sampler must decide whether a missed observation reflects equipment, weather, access, method, or an unresolved signal.

## Failure / stress expression

- A dead battery, damaged trap, or wet field route produces a delayed sample.
- Different methods or time windows make observations difficult to compare.
- A laboratory handoff is delayed by chain-of-custody, staffing, or equipment work.
- Privacy, legitimacy, or wording disputes delay release of an aggregate notice.

These expressions concern observability and stewardship. They do not establish incidence, transmission, exposure, dose, infection, or disease.

## Governance / institutional interface

Who observes: field teams, laboratories, environmental observers, and public-health surveillance staff. Who decides: authorized health, environmental, or program offices within their mandates. Who maintains: field crews, laboratory, fleet, facility, and data staff. Who owns: sample and data stewardship arrangements vary; future ownership is unresolved. Who regulates: public-health, environmental, laboratory, privacy, and safety authorities. Who communicates: program offices, data stewards, and public-information staff. A signal is not authority or control.

## Sensory environment

Wetland insects move through reeds and humid air. Rain taps a field vehicle roof. A cooler lid snaps shut beside a muddy access track. Laboratory refrigerator hum, battery alarms, and phone alerts sit beside frogs, wind, and the sound of tires on gravel.

## Regime variation

### A — Coordinated Technological Adaptation

Shared methods, provenance, staffed laboratories, privacy-preserving exchange, and regular communication make the sample-to-notice chain auditable. Human interpretation remains explicit.

### B — Uneven Networked Modernization

### C — High Capability / High Friction Basin

## Uncertainty / non-claims

The repository does not support exact sampling coverage, local attribution, incidence, transmission, vector abundance, human contact, infection, disease, exposure, dose, health outcome, deployment map, or response success. A signal is not incidence, detection is not establishment, and response capacity is not absence of disease. This is S material, not canon.

## A normal operating day

A technician checks the access log, room temperature, power-conditioning status, and spare-parts shelf. A compute operator reports a task competing with maintenance. The technician replaces a filter or cable, confirms fallback, and an energy operator agrees a timing adjustment. A data steward reviews the audit record; software is not authorization.

## Artifacts and material culture

- maintenance door, access reader, and identity log
- power-conditioning units, battery cabinets, and backup alarms
- cooling filters, ducts, coolant lines, and service ladders
- racks, spare modules, seals, and tool cases
- paper or local fallback procedures


## Maintenance and repair

Filters, coolant, batteries, cables, authentication hardware, seals, servers, and power equipment follow different lifecycles. Dust, heat, vibration, restricted access, and delayed patches turn routine service into coordinated work. A technician judges whether a warning is a sensor, cooling, power, or data-use problem. Local fallback must be exercised, not merely stored.

## Failure / stress expression

- Energy timing and compute scheduling conflict during a maintenance or operating window.
- A cooling, battery, cable, or authentication component needs replacement while access is restricted.
- A patch, spare module, or compatible standard is delayed.
- A data-use dispute pauses a coordination function even though the room has power and compute.

These expressions show dependency and friction; they do not imply automatic collapse or a guaranteed service interruption.

## Governance / institutional interface

Who observes: facility technicians, compute operators, energy operators, identity staff, and auditors. Who decides: authorized facility, energy, information, or governance officials. Who maintains: facility crews, compute technicians, power and cooling workers, and vendors. Who owns: room, equipment, data, and contracts may differ; future ownership is unresolved. Who regulates: energy, safety, privacy, cyber, labor, and public authorities. Who communicates: facility managers, data stewards, service owners, and recourse offices. Software recommendation is not dispatch, legal, or civic authority.

## Sensory environment

Fan noise and warm air fill the service corridor. Indicator lights reflect off steel and dark glass. A backup alarm interrupts the equipment hum. Outside, rail vibration, lake-weathered concrete, and humid air continue.

## Regime variation

### A — Coordinated Technological Adaptation

Energy, facility, information, labor, and governance teams use shared audits, planned replacement, tested fallback, and explicit authorization. The room remains accountable outside itself.

### B — Uneven Networked Modernization

Some rooms use compatible identity, energy, and maintenance interfaces while others depend on local records, adapters, and vendor parts. Fallback varies with staffing and access.

### C — High Capability / High Friction Basin

Selected rooms contain substantial compute while procurement, cyber trust, cooling access, labor, or public accountability remains contested. Capacity can coexist with delayed authorization or disputed data use.
