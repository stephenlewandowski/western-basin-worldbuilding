# Glasspunk Toledo / Northwest Ohio Sci-Fi Worldbuilding Project

**Working title:** Glasspunk Toledo: A Great Lakes Future Atlas  
**Core location:** Toledo, Ohio / Northwest Ohio / Lake Erie / Maumee River region  
**Initial time horizon:** ~2050–2080, expandable backward to 2026 and forward to 2100+  
**Project type:** Place-based speculative fiction, scenario atlas, science-informed worldbuilding, and eventual short-story / game / illustrated setting project.

---

## 1. Core Premise

Glasspunk Toledo imagines Northwest Ohio as a revived Great Lakes region where freshwater security, advanced glass and materials, environmental restoration, defense research, skilled trades, universities, and autonomous infrastructure converge.

The setting blends:

- Solarpunk optimism
- Cyberpunk systems and surveillance
- Steampunk / industrial heritage aesthetics
- Great Lakes climate adaptation
- Rust Belt revival
- Public health and environmental science
- Military and national-security futures
- Real geography, infrastructure, and local culture

The goal is not simply to predict future gadgets. The goal is to explore how real places, institutions, workers, families, ecosystems, and communities adapt when technologies such as AI, drones, fusion, biotechnology, exposomics, quantum systems, and remote sensing become embedded in daily life.

**Guiding idea:**

> The future arrived everywhere, but Toledo made it physical.

---

## 2. Distinctive Identity: “Glasspunk”

Glasspunk should feel different from generic cyberpunk. Its visual and cultural language comes from Toledo’s actual history and geography.

Key imagery:

- Transparent solar glass
- Art glass and smart glass architecture
- Brick factories converted into research labs
- Restored wetlands and river corridors
- High-level bridge as a civic icon
- Lake Erie sensor buoys and autonomous patrol craft
- Quarries repurposed for data, energy, storage, or research
- Jeep legacy vehicles, expedition robotics, and rugged autonomous platforms
- Mayfly blooms illuminated by city lights and drone traffic
- Industrial machinery mixed with ecological restoration
- Fusion/nuclear-powered data centers and manufacturing districts
- Water-intake infrastructure treated as strategic civic infrastructure

Tone:

- Hopeful but not naïve
- Technically serious
- Politically complex
- Regionally grounded
- Ecologically weird
- Institutionally believable
- Human-centered

## 3. Regional Structure

A simplified map can divide Northwest Ohio into 5–6 regions. Each region should have a unique culture, industries, resources, political identity, and historical memory.

### 3.1 Glass City Core

**Center:** Downtown Toledo and nearby urban districts  
**Identity:** Advanced glass, photonics, AI, finance, architecture, governance  
**Industries:**

- Smart glass
- Photonic computing
- Transparent displays
- Optical sensing
- Environmental data platforms
- Civic AI systems

**Narrative function:** Political center, research-commercial hub, data governance conflicts.

---

### 3.2 Maumee River Commons

**Center:** Maumee River corridor inland from Toledo  
**Identity:** River commerce, ecological restoration, agriculture, water engineering  
**Industries:**

- Floating markets
- Autonomous river freight
- Wetland restoration finance
- Agricultural runoff management
- Algal bloom monitoring
- Ecosystem services trading

**Narrative function:** Water politics, farm-city conflict, restoration disputes.

---

### 3.3 Nuclear / Energy Coast

**Center:** Lake Erie shoreline, power sites, data centers, port-adjacent zones  
**Identity:** Clean energy, strategic infrastructure, freshwater security  
**Industries:**

- Advanced nuclear / modular reactors
- Possible fusion research or strategic fusion deployment
- Hydrogen production
- Data centers
- Desalination / purification technology
- Grid-scale energy storage

**Narrative function:** Power, compute, water intake, defense, and infrastructure risk.

---

### 3.4 Black Swamp Preserve

**Center:** Rewilded / restored portions of former Black Swamp landscape  
**Identity:** Biodiversity, carbon farming, insects, restoration ecology  
**Industries:**

- Biodiversity banking

- Ecological tourism
- Biotech field stations
- Pollinator and insect research

**Narrative function:** Mayflies, mosquitoes, invasive species, ecological weirdness, nonhuman futures.

- Precision agriculture

### 5.1 Frameworks to Include

- Future of exposure science
- Environmental justice mapping

- Exposome / exposomics

- Remote sensing and space technology
- GIS and scenario modeling
- Defense health and military exposure records

### 5.2 Exposome / Exposomics

Incorporate exposome advancement, including work associated with Andrea Baccarelli and Gary Miller.

Core idea:

> The future of public health moves from measuring pollutants in places to reconstructing lifetime exposure histories in bodies, communities, occupations, and ecosystems.

Possible future concepts:

- Lifetime exposure records
- Individual longitudinal exposure ledgers
- Military exposure histories
- Epigenetic aging and environmental biomarkers
- Personal dose histories
- Neighborhood exposure scores
- Occupational exposure passports
- Wearable environmental sensors
- Public-health AI models
- Exposure cartography as a profession

### 5.3 Air Pollution and Environmental Justice

Air pollution should be a major recurring theme.

Pollution sources and themes:

- Legacy coal power plants
- Coal ash and industrial residue
- Vehicle exhaust
- Diesel corridors
- Port emissions
- Freight and rail emissions
- Farm dust
- Pesticide drift
- Quarries and limestone dust
- Industrial glass/metals manufacturing emissions
- Wildfire smoke
- Ozone and PM2.5
- Algal bloom aerosols
- Environmental justice and social vulnerability

The user has co-authored a position paper on air pollution environmental justice, which can provide authentic conceptual grounding for this part of the setting.

## 6. Neighborhood Exposure Score

One of the strongest project mechanics.

A future Great Lakes Exposure Index / Neighborhood Exposure Score can function as:

- A public health tool
- A political weapon
- A real estate force
- A school district controversy
- An insurance and liability issue
- A military/veterans’ claims tool
- A research dataset
- A source of discrimination
- A driver of migration and neighborhood identity

### 6.1 Possible Score Components

| Component | Possible Weight |
|---|---:|
| Chronic air pollution: PM2.5, ozone, diesel, industrial emissions | 30% |
| Episodic air events: wildfire smoke, pesticide drift, farm dust, algal aerosols | 20% |
| Legacy contamination: coal ash, metals, brownfields, quarries | 15% |
| Climate stress: heat, flooding, humidity, lake ice instability | 15% |
| Mobility / occupation correction: commute routes, job exposure, school/sports activity | 10% |
| Social vulnerability / mitigation capacity | 10% |

### 6.2 Evolution Over Time

| Era | Exposure System | Political Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 2020s | EJScreen-style public mapping | Grants, advocacy, litigation |
| 2030s | Satellite, local sensors, QGIS, open data | Climate resilience planning |
| 2040s | Wearable exposure devices | Personal exposure enters health care |
| 2050s | Exposome-linked biomarkers | Liability, insurance, military claims |
| 2060s–2070s | Civic exposure infrastructure | Scores shape housing, schools, jobs, politics |

### 6.3 Story Uses

- Exposure redlining
- Sensor justice movements
- Wearable sensor fraud
- Pesticide drift lawsuits
- School athletics restrictions during ozone/heat/pesticide events
- Veterans proving service-connected exposure
- Corporate undercounting of industrial exposure
- Insurance discrimination
- Neighborhoods contesting official scores
- UT researchers caught between science, politics, and funding

## 10. Story Engines

### 10.1 Core Story Formula

Each story should combine:

1. One person
2. One job
3. One place
4. One institutional conflict
5. One speculative technology or scientific framework
6. One personal wound/desire
7. One decision that cannot be avoided

### 10.2 Example Premises

#### The Exposure Cartographer

A UT-trained exposure cartographer discovers that a restored wetland generating millions in ecosystem-service credits is hiding a defense sensor array and falsified algal bloom data.

#### The Water Intake Alarm

During a major mayfly bloom, Toledo’s water-intake AI says the water is safe. Wearable sensors and insect behavior suggest otherwise. A retired Army environmental health scientist must decide whether to trigger a shutdown.

#### Friday Night Ozone

A high school football rivalry game is threatened by ozone, pesticide drift, and heat-index restrictions. Parents, coaches, AI health models, and scouts collide.

#### Camp Perry Ghost Range

Autonomous drones over Lake Erie begin mimicking old firearms competition patterns from Camp Perry. Nobody knows whether it is a training algorithm, a hack, or a memorial.

#### Quarry Memory

A limestone quarry converted into an underground data center begins producing anomalous heat and exposure signatures. A maintenance apprentice finds legacy contamination records that were never digitized.

#### The Mayfly Index

The annual mayfly bloom is used as a biodiversity success metric, but a sensor justice group claims the insects are carrying traces of an experimental exposure biomarker.

#### Michigan Water Compact

Toledo and Michigan enter a dispute over autonomous water routing, lake sensor jurisdiction, football rivalry propaganda, and freshwater sovereignty.

## 14. Craft Development Plan

### 14.1 Main Craft Risk

The worldbuilding may become more interesting than the stories.

Solution:

> Begin each story with a person under pressure, not a lecture about the world.

### 14.2 Practice Method

#### Week 1: Read Like a Mechanic

For each story/chapter read, record:

- Protagonist
- Desire
- Obstacle
- Speculative element
- Final decision

#### Week 2: Four Short Sketches

Write four 1,000-word Glasspunk sketches.

Rules:

- One person
- One job
- One bad day
- No exposition dump
- Only necessary worldbuilding

#### Week 3: Expand One Sketch

Revise the strongest sketch into a 3,000–5,000 word story.

#### Week 4: Reader Feedback

Ask readers:

1. Where were you confused?
2. Where were you bored?
3. What image or idea stuck with you?
